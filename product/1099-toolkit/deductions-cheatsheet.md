### The 90% rule

If 90% of your business income comes from one or two clients and you work like an employee, the IRS may classify some of your "deductions" differently. Most freelancers don't hit this line — but if you do, get a CPA review.

### Categories where freelancers leave the most money on the table

These are the deductions freelancers most often forget to claim.

| What | Where it goes | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|
| **Home office** | Form 8829 | Skipping it because they think it triggers audits. It doesn't, if you qualify. The space must be used regularly *and* exclusively for business. |
| **Health insurance premiums** | Schedule 1, Line 17 | Deductible up to your net SE income. Not on Schedule C — it's an "above the line" adjustment. |
| **Solo 401(k) / SEP-IRA contributions** | Schedule 1, Line 16 | A solo 401(k) can shelter up to $23,000 of salary deferral plus 25% of net SE income (2025 limits — verify current year). One of the biggest tax moves a freelancer can make. |
| **Phone & internet (business %)** | Schedule C, Line 27a | Most people use their phone for both — deduct the business-use portion. |
| **Continuing education** | Schedule C, Line 27a | Courses, books, conferences related to your existing trade are deductible. New-trade education is not. |
| **Software & subscriptions** | Schedule C, Line 27a | Adobe, Canva, Notion, ChatGPT Plus, LinkedIn Premium — all deductible if used for the business. |
| **Business meals** | Schedule C, Line 24b | 50% deductible. Note who was there and the business purpose, every time. |
| **Mileage** | Schedule C, Line 9 | Use the standard mileage rate (verify current year on IRS.gov) — *not* actual gas + maintenance — unless you really track everything. Log every trip. |
| **Self-employment tax deduction** | Schedule 1, Line 15 | You deduct half of your SE tax automatically. Most tax software does this; if you DIY, don't forget. |

### Categories that freelancers wrongly claim

If you don't actually qualify, deducting these is what triggers audits.

| What | Why it's risky |
|---|---|
| **Commuting miles** | Driving from home to a regular workplace is *not* deductible. Only travel between business locations or to a client. |
| **Entertainment** | Tickets to games, concerts, golf — no longer deductible since the 2017 tax law change. |
| **Clothing** | A suit is only deductible if it's a uniform that can't be worn in daily life. A nice shirt for client meetings doesn't qualify. |
| **Personal phone purchases** | If you buy a new iPhone and use it 70% personally, only 30% is deductible. Most people overstate this. |
| **Vague "Misc. business expense"** | If you can't articulate the category and the business purpose, expect to lose it in an audit. |

### Documentation rules

For every deduction, the IRS wants you to be able to answer four questions:

1. **What** did you buy?
2. **When** did you buy it?
3. **How much** did it cost?
4. **What was the business purpose?**

A receipt with date and amount handles 1–3. You have to write the business purpose somewhere — that's why the Expense Tracker in this toolkit has a "Description" column. Use it.

### When to stop reading and call a CPA

- Net SE income > $80,000 (you should be in an S-corp election conversation, not a sole-prop conversation)
- You have multi-state income (clients in more than one state)
- You're behind on quarterly estimated taxes by more than one quarter
- You got an IRS notice — even a small one
- You bought a vehicle, equipment, or rental property this year
- You're considering hiring someone
